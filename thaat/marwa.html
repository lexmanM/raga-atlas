Welcome to Rāga Atlas

About Marwa

Marwa is thāṭ 6 of the ten Hindustani parent scales: Sa, komal Re, Ga, tivra Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni. Rāgas filed under it here: Marwa, Sohini.

Primer

How rāgas are organised

Five ideas, in plain language. No notation required.

01

An octave has twelve doors

Pick any note and sing until you reach the same note again, higher. Between those two points sit twelve usable pitches — the same twelve on a piano or a harmonium. Hindustani music calls each one a swar.

02

Seven names, twelve shades

The seven names are Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni — written S R G M P D N. Sa is home and Pa never moves. Re, Ga, Dha and Ni each have a natural form, shuddha, and a lowered one, komal, written with a line underneath. Ma goes the other way: its altered form is raised, tīvra, written with a stroke above.

03

A rāga is a path, not a scale

A rāga specifies the way up — the āroha — and the way down — the avaroha. They need not match. Each rāga also has a pakaḍ, a short catch-phrase that gives it away within a few notes. A dot under a letter means the octave below; a dot above, the octave above.

04

Ten thāṭs are the filing system

Early in the twentieth century Bhatkhande sorted the rāgas under ten parent scales called thāṭs, each with all seven names. A thāṭ is a shelf, not a song — nobody performs one — and the fit is loose: some rāgas borrow a note their thāṭ does not have.

05

Two notes rule, and the clock matters

Every rāga has a vādī, the note it keeps returning to, and a samvādī, its second in command. Two rāgas with the same notes can differ only in which ones they lean on. Each rāga also belongs to a time: the day is cut into eight three-hour watches, prahars, and a rāga is at home in one of them.
